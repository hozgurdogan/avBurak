Severance pay is not owed in every case where an employment contract ends - it is owed only in the situations and under the conditions listed by law. This article summarises, for general information, the conditions for entitlement to severance pay.

## The common condition: at least one year of service

To be entitled to severance pay, the employee must have worked for at least **one year** at the same employer's workplace or workplaces. This period is calculated under article 14 of the former Labour Law No. 1475 (kept in force specifically for severance pay), which remains the core legal basis for severance pay.

## In which types of termination is severance pay owed

- **Termination by the employer without just cause:** if the employer ends the employment contract without a just cause listed in the Labour Law - such as conduct contrary to rules of morality and good faith - the employee is entitled to severance pay.
- **Termination by the employee for just cause:** an employee may claim severance pay if they terminate the contract based on a just cause listed by law, such as unpaid wages, health reasons, or the employer's conduct contrary to rules of morality and good faith.
- **Retirement, disability, or death:** severance pay is owed if the employee leaves voluntarily after completing the insured service period and premium payment days required for an old-age, retirement, or disability pension, or in the event of the employee's death.
- **Termination by a female employee due to marriage:** a female employee who resigns voluntarily within one year of her marriage date is entitled to severance pay.
- **Compulsory military service:** severance pay is also owed if the employee leaves due to compulsory military service.

## When severance pay is not owed

Severance pay is not owed if the employer terminates the contract for just cause based on the employee's conduct contrary to rules of morality and good faith listed in article 25/II of the Labour Law (for example theft, breach of trust, unexcused absence). Similarly, an employee's voluntary resignation without any just cause does not, as a rule, give rise to a right to severance pay.

## Basis of calculation and the statutory cap

Severance pay is calculated as 30 days' worth of the employee's last gross "dressed" wage (the wage plus regularly paid benefits such as bonuses, transport, and meals) for each full year of service; periods exceeding a full year are pro-rated. The wage used in the calculation cannot exceed the statutory severance pay cap, which is updated annually.

## Common mistakes

- Basing the calculation only on the bare gross wage, without including regularly paid benefits such as bonuses, meals, or transport.
- Failing to correctly assess whether the ground for termination truly qualifies as just cause - for example, whether low performance alone can constitute just cause.
- A female employee missing the one-year window to exercise her right to resign due to marriage.
- Confusing severance pay with notice pay; these are two different entitlements with different conditions and calculation methods.
