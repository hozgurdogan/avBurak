For a debtor who has been served with a payment order from the enforcement office, the first question is usually: how many days do I have, and what happens if I don't object? This article covers, for general information, the time limit for objecting to a payment order in judgment-free enforcement proceedings, the effect of an objection, and the routes available to the creditor afterwards.

## What a payment order is

When a creditor wants to collect a monetary claim without having a court judgment in hand (through judgment-free enforcement), an application is made to the enforcement office, which sends the debtor a payment order. The order states the amount of the debt, the document it is based on, and the debtor's rights.

## The seven-day objection period

Under article 62 of Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law No. 2004, the debtor may object to the enforcement office **within seven days** of the date the payment order is served. The objection may go to the whole or part of the debt, to the creditor's standing to enforce, or to the signature. This period is short and non-extendable; missing it allows the proceeding to become final.

## The effect of an objection: the proceeding stops automatically

A timely, properly made objection **automatically halts the proceeding**, with no further action needed. For the proceeding to continue, the creditor must take further steps; an objected-to proceeding does not move to the seizure stage unless the creditor acts.

## Two routes available to the creditor

- **Removal of the objection:** if the creditor holds a document of the kind required by the Enforcement and Bankruptcy Law (for example, a private deed whose signature is acknowledged, a negotiable instrument, or a notarial deed), an application can be made to the enforcement court to have the objection removed. This is a relatively fast route, based on a limited review.
- **Annulment of the objection:** the creditor may file an action for annulment of the objection before the general court within one year of the objection being served. This is a fuller proceeding in which the claim is examined on the merits.

Which route applies depends on the nature of the document held and the basis of the claim; choosing between them without assessing the specific file is not advisable.

## Common mistakes

- Miscalculating the date the payment order was served and missing the seven-day period.
- Failing to state clearly in the objection petition what it is directed at (the whole debt, part of it, the signature, or standing to enforce).
- Assuming, once an objection has been filed, that the proceeding has ended for good, and overlooking that the creditor may still pursue removal or annulment of the objection.
- Not considering, when objecting to the debt, that a negative-declaration action may also be available.

## Competent authority

The objection to a payment order is filed directly with the enforcement office handling the proceeding. A request for removal of the objection goes to the enforcement court; an action for annulment of the objection is filed with the competent general court (such as a civil or commercial court of first instance) depending on the nature of the claim. The process can vary with the specific facts of the file.
