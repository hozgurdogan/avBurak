For foreign investors, the first question about establishing a company in Türkiye is usually structural rather than legal: which corporate form, which capital structure and which management model fit the activity that is actually planned? This article outlines the incorporation process under the Turkish Commercial Code no. 6102 and the Foreign Direct Investment Law no. 4875, together with the practical stages of the procedure and the procedural questions that arise most often. It is written for general information.

## Equal treatment and the legal framework

The organising principle of Foreign Direct Investment Law no. 4875 is that foreign investors are treated on the same footing as domestic investors. Subject to international agreements and to provisions of special legislation, the law imposes no prior authorisation and no minimum capital requirement specific to foreign direct investment. The practical consequence is straightforward: a foreign natural or legal person may establish any company type available to Turkish nationals, following the same procedure and the same minimum capital.

The exceptions to this general rule are sectoral. Special legislation governing banking, insurance, financial leasing, payment services, capital markets activities, radio and television broadcasting, aviation, maritime transport and mining may impose additional conditions in terms of licensing, shareholding limits or minimum capital. Reviewing those sectoral rules before the field of activity is fixed avoids having to restructure the company after incorporation.

## Choosing the corporate form

In practice, most foreign-owned investments are structured either as a limited liability company or as a joint stock company.

**A limited liability company** may be formed with a single shareholder and may not exceed fifty shareholders. The minimum share capital was increased by an amendment in 2024 and should be confirmed against the current communiqué of the Ministry of Trade at the time of incorporation. Transfer of shares requires a written instrument, notarisation of the signatures and, as a rule, approval by the general assembly. Because the transfer of participation shares is subject to these formalities, the form suits investments where a small and stable shareholder group is intended.

**A joint stock company** may likewise be formed with a single shareholder, and its shares are in principle freely transferable; the transfer of registered shares is not subject to any approval unless the articles of association restrict it. The minimum capital is higher than for a limited liability company, and a separate minimum applies to non-public companies that adopt the registered capital system. At least twenty-five per cent of the subscribed cash capital must be paid before registration, with the balance payable within twenty-four months. Where the plan involves admitting investors, staged capital increases, future share transfers or share option arrangements, the joint stock company offers the more flexible framework.

A branch and a liaison office are further options. A branch has no separate legal personality but is registered with the trade registry, and the liability of the parent company continues. A liaison office may not carry out commercial activity at all; it is opened with the permission of the Ministry of Trade for activities such as market research, representation and promotion, and the permission is granted for a limited term.

## The incorporation process

In practice the process consists of the following consecutive stages:

1. **Obtaining potential tax numbers.** Foreign shareholders and foreign board members obtain a potential tax identification number from the tax office. This number is a precondition for opening a bank account and for the registration itself.
2. **Preparing and legalising documents.** Foreign individual shareholders provide a copy of their passport; foreign corporate shareholders provide a certificate of activity, a resolution of the competent corporate body and a signature circular. Documents issued abroad must bear an apostille under the 1961 Hague Convention, to which Türkiye is a party, or be legalised by a Turkish consulate where the issuing state is not a party. Translation by a sworn translator and notarisation of the translation are required.
3. **MERSIS application.** The articles of association are drawn up electronically through the Central Registry Record System, and a MERSIS number is allocated to the company.
4. **Signature approval.** Founder signatures are approved before the trade registry directorate or a notary. Founders who are abroad may be represented under a duly executed and apostilled power of attorney.
5. **Depositing capital and the competition levy.** The portion of cash capital required by law is deposited with a bank and blocked. A levy of 0.04 per cent of the capital is also paid to the Competition Authority.
6. **Registration and announcement.** The company acquires legal personality upon registration with the trade registry, and the registration is published in the Turkish Trade Registry Gazette.
7. **Post-registration steps.** Certification of the opening of commercial books, the tax office inspection, registration for corporate income tax and value added tax, enrolment for electronic notification and, where applicable, electronic invoicing, issuance of the signature circular and any sectoral licences are completed at this stage.

Where documents are complete and properly legalised, registration is usually concluded quickly; the timetable is generally driven by the apostille and translation of documents issued abroad rather than by the registry itself.

## Management, representation and work permits

A limited liability company must have at least one manager authorised to represent it, and at least one shareholder must be appointed as a manager with the right of management and the power of representation. A joint stock company is represented by its board of directors. Directors and managers need not be resident in Türkiye. However, foreign managers and staff who will actually work in Türkiye require a work permit. Work permit applications are assessed against criteria concerning the company's paid-in capital, turnover or export volume, together with conditions on the ratio of Turkish to foreign employees. Specific conditions as to the amount and percentage of shareholding may apply to foreign managers who are also shareholders.

## Continuing obligations after the investment

Information notifications to the Ministry of Trade in respect of foreign direct investment exist so that capital movements and share transfers can be monitored. Beyond that, holding general assemblies within the statutory period, preparing financial statements, appointing an auditor where the company is subject to independent audit, and complying with transfer pricing and thin capitalisation rules are matters that require continuing attention after incorporation.

## Assessment

The act of incorporation is technically a simple one. What matters is whether the chosen structure fits the medium-term plan for the investment. Where the prospect of additional shareholders, capital increases, share transfers, a shareholders' agreement and exit scenarios is considered at the incorporation stage, the need for later structural change — and the cost that comes with it — is reduced.
